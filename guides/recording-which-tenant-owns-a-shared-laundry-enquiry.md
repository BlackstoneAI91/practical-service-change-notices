# Recording Who Owns a Laundry Enquiry When Two Tenants Share the Service

A practical guide for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers on recording who owns a shared laundry enquiry when two tenants use one building, including a blank handoff record and sample acknowledgement sentences.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why a shared enquiry needs a named owner

Picture an invented Sarawak building, which we will call the Shared Service House throughout this guide. Two tenants occupy it: a weekend events tenant and a weekday training tenant. Both use the same laundry service arrangement, and both have questions. One asks about towels used at weekend functions. The other asks about table linen used in weekday sessions. Neither question is difficult on its own. The difficulty is that both tenants may write to the same provider, sometimes on the same day, and the provider cannot tell from two separate messages whether it is answering one request or two.

A named owner solves a narrow but important problem: it tells everyone who is allowed to explain the request, who may approve its scope, and who should be copied rather than asked to decide. Without that, a provider may receive two slightly different descriptions of the same need and have to guess which one is current.

This guide is about recording responsibility, not about treatment methods, prices or bookings. It is written for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers who share a service arrangement and want their notes to stay consistent. ## Three roles, kept separate

The most common mistake in a shared enquiry is to treat ownership as a single label. In practice, three roles need to be distinguished.

The explainer is the person who can describe the request accurately. They know what is being sent, when it is used, and what changed since last time. They may not have authority to agree anything.

The approver is the person who may confirm scope on behalf of the tenant. They can say yes to a list of items or a description of the work, and they can say no. They may not know the operational detail.

The contact is the person the provider should reply to. Often this is the explainer, but not always. If the explainer is away, the contact should be someone who can still reach the approver.

Writing these three names down, with the date, prevents the quiet drift that happens when one person answers a question they were not authorised to answer. It also gives the provider a clear instruction about who to ask when something is unclear.

For a household, the same three roles may sit with two people. For an accommodation operator or venue manager, they are more likely to be split across a coordinator and a manager. The record does not need to be elaborate. It needs to be current.

## What the provider should clarify before notes are combined

When two tenants share an enquiry, the provider is often asked to respond to a combined set of notes. Before treating those notes as one request, it helps to clarify a few practical points.

First, who is the single point of contact for this enquiry? A provider that receives messages from two people needs to know which reply is authoritative.

Second, which items or areas does the enquiry actually cover? A combined note may mix laundry items with other cleaning needs. ## A blank handoff record you can copy

The record below is a template, not a form issued by any provider. Adapt the fields to your building and keep one copy where both tenants can see it.

Shared laundry enquiry handoff record

Building or site name: ______________________
Date opened: ______________________
Enquiry reference (your own): ______________________

Tenant A name: ______________________
Explainer: ______________________
Approver: ______________________
Contact for provider replies: ______________________

Tenant B name: ______________________
Explainer: ______________________
Approver: ______________________
Contact for provider replies: ______________________

Single point of contact for this enquiry: ______________________
Services or categories mentioned (for example laundry, dry cleaning, curtain cleaning, carpet cleaning): ______________________
Items or areas described: ______________________
What changed since the last enquiry: ______________________

Approval status: not yet seen / seen by both approvers / one approver only
Date approval recorded: ______________________

Notes combined by: ______________________
Date notes combined: ______________________
Documents shared with tenants (quotation, written confirmation, terms): ______________________

Next review date: ______________________

Two habits make this record useful. First, fill in the approval status honestly; a blank field is better than a guessed one. Second, date every change. A record without dates cannot show which version the provider actually received.

## Two sample acknowledgement sentences

When the record is complete, a short acknowledgement tells the provider what it is receiving. These are examples, not required wording.

Example one, for a combined note sent by one tenant on behalf of both: "This note combines the laundry enquiry from both tenants in the building; Tenant A's approver has seen it, Tenant B's approver has not yet, and replies should come to the contact named in the record."

Example two, for a note sent before approval is complete: "Please treat this as a draft enquiry only; the scope has been described by both tenants but not yet approved by either approver, and we will confirm in writing before asking you to act on it."

Both sentences do one job: they separate description from approval. That separation is the whole point of the record. A provider reading either sentence knows whether it is being asked to review or to proceed.

For households sharing a service, a shorter version works: "This enquiry covers items from two households; I am the contact, and the other household has seen the list but has not confirmed it."

## Keeping the record current across a tenancy change

The record is most likely to fail at a changeover: a new coordinator, a new tenant, or a new contact at the provider. Three small habits reduce that risk.

Review the record when a named person changes, not only when the enquiry changes. A record that still lists a departed coordinator as the approver is worse than no record, because it looks authoritative.

Keep one master copy. Two tenants editing separate copies will produce two versions, and the provider may receive either. A single shared document with a visible date is enough.

Close the record when the enquiry ends. Note the date and whether the outcome was confirmed in writing. A closed record is easier to reopen than a stale one.

## A short checklist before you send

The following checklist is an author heuristic, not a standard or a certification. Score it however you like; the aim is to catch gaps before the provider does.

Is one person named as the single point of contact? Is each tenant's explainer and approver recorded separately? Is the approval status honest, including any approver who has not yet seen the note? Are the service categories named rather than implied? Is the date of the combined note recorded? Has the provider been told whether it is reviewing a draft or an approved scope? Are the relevant terms and written confirmations noted as documents shared, without treating them as advice?

If any answer is no, the record is not ready to send. That is not a failure; it is the record doing its job. A shared laundry enquiry is a small piece of coordination, and writing down who owns which part is usually faster than untangling two versions later.
