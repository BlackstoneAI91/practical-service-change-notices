# Updating an App Requirement Brief When the Sign-Off Owner Changes

A practical, nontechnical guide for Malaysian service businesses on revising an app requirement brief when a new staff role takes over sign-off: keep the original approval, record the current owner by role, and hand over with a copyable note.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why a Sign-Off Change Is a Brief Change

An app requirement brief is not only a list of features. It records who agreed to what, when, and on what basis. When the person or role responsible for sign-off changes, the brief can quietly become misleading: the document still names an approver who has moved on, while the new owner inherits decisions they never saw.

The safest approach is to treat the change as an amendment, not a rewrite. Keep the original approval intact, add the new owner by role, and record the handover in a short note that travels with the brief. This preserves the audit trail and gives the incoming owner a fair starting point.

For a fictional example, imagine a Malaysian service business building a booking app. The operations manager approved the first requirement set. Six months later, a newly created service delivery lead role takes over sign-off. The brief should still show the operations manager's original approval, then show the service delivery lead as the current sign-off owner from a stated date.

This is the kind of documentation discipline that matters in any software application development company engagement, because requirements often outlive the people who first agreed to them.

## What to Keep, What to Add, What to Retire

Before editing anything, sort the existing brief into three groups.

Keep unchanged: the original approval record, the date it was given, the version number it applied to, and any conditions attached to that approval. These are historical facts and should not be overwritten.

Add: the new sign-off owner's role title, the date responsibility transferred, the scope of what they now approve, and any limits on that authority. If the new owner approves only design changes but not budget changes, say so.

Retire carefully: old contact names, personal email addresses and informal approval messages should be removed from the working copy but noted in the change log. Do not delete the original approval; mark it as superseded for future decisions while keeping it visible for history.

A simple status label helps. Use terms such as approved, approved with conditions, pending re-confirmation, or superseded. Avoid vague labels like done or okay, which tell a reader nothing about what was actually agreed.

If your organisation runs several systems at once, a consistent labelling habit across projects makes handovers far easier to compare.

## Record the Owner by Role, Not by Name Alone

Names change; roles persist. The brief should identify the current sign-off owner primarily by role title, with the person's name as a secondary detail.

A workable pattern is: Role title, department, date responsibility began, scope of sign-off authority, and the name of the person currently holding the role. When the person changes again, only the name and date need updating.

This matters in Malaysian service businesses where a single staff member may cover several functions. If the sign-off owner is also the person who tests the app or manages the vendor relationship, note those overlapping responsibilities so a future reader understands why one role appears in several places.

Where a vendor is involved, the brief should also state who on the vendor side receives approvals and who on the client side gives them. Ambiguity here is a common source of delay, because each side may assume the other is waiting.

Keep the language plain. A brief that a nontechnical manager can read without a glossary is more likely to be maintained than one written for specialists only.

## A Copyable Handover Note

The handover note can be short. Its job is to explain the change to anyone who reads the brief later, including people who never met the previous owner.

Example, for the fictional booking app:

Handover note
Project: Service booking app (internal reference only)
Brief version affected: 1.3
Previous sign-off owner: Operations Manager, approved 14 March, approved with conditions (condition: payment flow to be reviewed before build)
New sign-off owner: Service Delivery Lead, responsibility began 1 September
Scope of new authority: approves screen flows, wording and acceptance criteria; budget changes remain with the finance lead
Status of previous approval: retained for history; superseded for future decisions from 1 September
Open items carried forward: payment flow review still outstanding
Next review date: 15 October

Notice what the note does not do. It does not claim the new owner re-approved everything. It does not erase the earlier conditions. It does not invent a decision that was never made.

Adapt the fields to your own project, but keep the structure: what changed, who owns it now, what remains open, and when it will be reviewed.

## Handling Open Conditions and Unfinished Reviews

A change of sign-off owner often coincides with unfinished work. Conditions attached to the original approval may still be open, and the new owner needs to know which ones they are inheriting.

List each open condition with its original wording, the date it was raised, and its current state. If a condition has been met, record the evidence briefly rather than simply marking it closed. If it has not been met, say who is expected to act and by when.

Be careful not to let the new owner's arrival reset the clock on decisions that were already agreed. A new sign-off owner can reasonably ask for a walkthrough, but the brief should distinguish between re-confirmation of existing requirements and new requirements being introduced.

Where a requirement genuinely needs to change, add it as a new line with its own date and owner rather than editing the old line in place. This keeps the history readable and prevents disputes about what was agreed and when.

## A Short Checklist Before You Publish the Update

Before circulating the revised brief, run through a short check. The following is an author's checklist, not a certified standard, and the timings are examples only.

One: the original approval and its date are still visible. Two: the new owner is recorded by role, with a start date. Three: the scope of the new owner's authority is stated, including any limits. Four: open conditions are carried forward with their original wording. Five: the status label is specific rather than vague. Six: personal contact details have been removed from the working copy. Seven: the change log names who made the update and when. Eight: a review date is set, for example two weeks after circulation, so the new owner can raise questions.

If any item cannot be completed, note why in the change log rather than leaving a silent gap. A brief with an acknowledged gap is easier to trust than one that appears complete but is not.

Finally, tell the people affected. A short message to the project group explaining that the sign-off owner has changed, linking to the updated brief and naming the review date, prevents the confusion that usually follows an unannounced handover.
