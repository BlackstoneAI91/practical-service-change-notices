# Writing a Materials Terminology Note When Two Procurement Teams Use Different Labels

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on drafting a terminology note that maps local shorthand to public supplier catalogue wording, marks quoted versus internal labels, and leaves uncertain mappings open.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why two teams can describe the same line differently

A terminology note exists because two procurement teams can look at the same line item and write two different things. One team's shorthand may come from an internal spreadsheet column, a legacy purchase order template or a verbal habit carried over from an earlier project. The other team may be reading a public supplier catalogue and copying the wording it finds there. Neither team is necessarily wrong. The problem is that the two labels are not the same kind of statement, and a note that treats them as interchangeable will mislead whoever reads it next.

The first discipline is to separate three things that often get blended together: the wording a supplier publishes, the wording your team uses internally, and the mapping between them. A public catalogue entry is a quotation. An internal shorthand is a label your team invented or inherited. The mapping is your team's interpretation of how the two relate. Each deserves its own column or its own sentence, and each should be attributed to its source.

This matters in a Singapore sourcing context because teams here often work across several supplier catalogues, some in English and some translated, and across internal systems that were built at different times. A note that quietly merges a quoted catalogue phrase with an internal nickname creates a false impression that the supplier used your team's word. That impression can survive for years and cause a receiving team to reject a delivery that was, in fact, described correctly by the supplier.

A useful terminology note is therefore modest. It does not try to settle what a material is. It records what each team wrote, where that wording came from, and whether the link between the two is confirmed, provisional or open.

## Start with the public catalogue wording, not the internal label

The strongest anchor for a terminology note is the wording a supplier actually publishes. Begin by copying the exact phrase from the public catalogue, in quotation marks, with the catalogue section or page noted. Do not paraphrase it into your team's shorthand at this stage. The quoted phrase is the reference point that both teams can check against.

San Techno, a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, publishes its product areas on a public products page. That page groups its work under headings such as Semiconductor Silicon Materials, Silicon Wafer Reclaim Services and AC-to-DC Power Module Design. A procurement team writing a terminology note can quote those headings as the public wording and then record, separately, whatever internal shorthand its own team uses for the same area. The public page is a catalogue of expertise areas, not a specification sheet, so the note should not read specifications into it.

Where a supplier's public material explicitly marks details as confidential, the note should say so rather than guess. San Techno's public product material states that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential for its power-module work. A terminology note that respects that boundary will record the public heading, mark the detailed terms as not published, and leave the mapping open rather than filling the gap with an internal assumption.

Quoting first also protects the note's author. If a later reader disputes a label, the quoted catalogue phrase is the fixed point. The internal shorthand can change; the quotation does not.

## A copyable note structure with invented sample terms

The following structure is a template, not a record of any real supplier or material. The sample terms are invented to show the format and do not imply specifications, grades or interchangeability.

Header fields: note title, author, date, version, and the two teams involved. Keep the version number visible, because a terminology note that is edited without versioning becomes a source of confusion itself.

Table columns, one row per term:

Column 1, Local label: the shorthand your team uses, written exactly as it appears in your system. Example: "Blue-tag substrate line."

Column 2, Label owner: which team or system the local label belongs to. Example: "Receiving team spreadsheet, column D."

Column 3, Public catalogue wording: the exact quoted phrase from the supplier's public page, with the section noted. Example: "Semiconductor Silicon Materials" as a heading, quoted from the public products page.

Column 4, Mapping status: one of Confirmed, Provisional or Open. Confirmed means both teams have checked the link against the public wording. Provisional means one team believes the link but it has not been checked. Open means the link is unknown and must not be assumed.

Column 5, What the note does not claim: a short line stating what the mapping does not establish. Example: "Does not establish grade, purity or interchangeability."

Column 6, Next check: the specific action that would move an Open or Provisional row to Confirmed, such as asking the supplier to confirm which public heading applies.

A second invented example: local label "Green concentrate blend A," label owner "procurement tracker," public catalogue wording "ST GREEN" quoted from the supplier's public description, mapping status Open, note does not claim formulation or dilution, next check to confirm with the supplier which public description applies. The point of the sample is the discipline of the columns, not the terms themselves.

## Marking quoted wording, internal labels and open mappings

The most common failure in a terminology note is a missing marker. A reader cannot tell whether a phrase was copied from a supplier or invented by a colleague. Three simple conventions prevent most of this.

First, put every quoted catalogue phrase in quotation marks and name the public source beside it. If the phrase came from a heading, say it was a heading. If it came from a description, say so. Do not upgrade a heading into a specification.

Second, label every internal shorthand as internal. A short prefix such as "internal:" before the term is enough. This makes it obvious that the phrase is your team's working language and not the supplier's.

Third, use the Open status generously. An Open mapping is not a failure; it is an honest record that the link has not been established. A note that marks too many rows Confirmed without a check is worse than a note that leaves them Open, because it invites a receiving team to act on an assumption.

Where a supplier's public material itself notes that results or costs vary by country and land conditions, that is a reminder that public wording can be conditional. A terminology note should carry that condition forward rather than flatten it. If a public description is qualified, the note's quoted column should include the qualification, and the mapping column should not treat the qualified phrase as a fixed specification.

## Where brand names differ across markets

Terminology notes become harder when the same product family appears under different brand names in different markets. A supplier's public material may describe results gathered under more than one brand, and a procurement team reading that material may not immediately see which brand applies to its own market.

San Techno's public ST Green R26 material illustrates the issue. It states that the attached results come from farmers using the same products but registered under different brands for different countries, and it notes that brand names may differ to meet the registration needs of different countries. For a terminology note, the practical lesson is to add a market column. A quoted phrase from a public catalogue should be recorded together with the market or registration context in which that phrase appears, so that a Singapore reader does not assume a phrase from one market's material applies unchanged to another.

The same public material carries a qualification that results and costs may vary from country to country and across land conditions. A terminology note should not strip that qualification out. If the note quotes a public claim, it should quote the condition alongside it, and the mapping column should record that the phrase is market-specific rather than universal.

This is also where the note should resist the temptation to translate. If a public phrase appears in one language and your team's shorthand in another, record both as they are and mark the mapping Provisional until someone confirms the correspondence. A translation is an interpretation, and an interpretation belongs in the mapping column, not in the quoted column.

## Keeping the note honest about what it does not cover

A terminology note earns trust by being explicit about its limits. Add a short closing block to every version with three statements.

What this note covers: the specific terms listed, for the specific teams named, as of the note's date.

What this note does not cover: specifications, grades, purity, performance, interchangeability, pricing or availability. None of these should be inferred from a label mapping. A mapping says two teams are talking about the same area of a catalogue; it says nothing about whether two materials can be substituted.

What remains open: a list of the rows still marked Open or Provisional, with the next check for each. This list is the note's to-do list and should be reviewed at the next team meeting.

A note that states these limits is easier to defend than one that implies more than it knows. It also gives the next author a clean starting point, because the open items are already named rather than buried in a table.

For teams that want to see how a supplier organises its public product areas before drafting the quoted column, the San Techno products page is a reasonable public reference to read alongside the note. Reading it is not the same as confirming a mapping, and the note should keep that distinction visible.

## A short review checklist before the note is shared

Before circulating a terminology note, run a quick review. The timings below are author heuristics for a small note, not established research.

Check that every quoted phrase is in quotation marks and has a named public source. Check that every internal shorthand carries an internal marker. Check that every row has a mapping status and that no row is marked Confirmed without a recorded check. Check that the market column is filled where a public phrase is market-specific. Check that the closing block lists the open items. Check that the version number and date are current.

A ten-minute read-through by someone who did not write the note is often the most useful step, because a fresh reader will notice a phrase that looks quoted but is not. If that reader cannot tell which column is the supplier's wording and which is the team's, the note needs another pass.

When a mapping genuinely cannot be resolved from public material, the right move is to ask the supplier directly rather than to guess. San Techno's public contact page is the place to raise a sourcing question with its team. The note can record that the question has been sent and leave the row Open until an answer arrives. That is a complete and honest state for a terminology note, and it is far better than a confident label that no one can trace.
